In this column we look at the extraordinary physics hiding behind everyday objects and phenomena. This time, we're staring — with our full attention — at a burning candle.

Ah, a candle at eye level. Few things in life are as hypnotic and fascinating as that flickering candlelight. Deep blue at the bottom, bright yellow at the top. It's a glowing marvel of nature that, despite the countless complex chemical and physical processes happening inside it, can stay unchanged and stable for hours on end. Fascinating, don't you think? What actually *is* a flame? I realize now that I don't actually know what a candle flame is. It can't be an object, that much is clear. The candle — that's an object. You can buy it in a shop, pick it up, move it around, turn it upside down. You can shine green light on it and then observe that your white candle now looks green. Those are all typical properties of an object.

No, a flame is different. Blow on it too hard and it disappears... into nothing. It does have colour, but a flame is also transparent at the same time. If you shine a lamp on a burning candle, you won't see a flame-shaped shadow. Spooky! In a fraction of a second, atoms fly through the flame, starting out in the hot candle wax and ending up as smoke tumbling upward. Those atoms aren't the flame itself, but they are part of it. A flame is a process, a self-sustaining chain reaction that needs a constant supply of new fuel and oxygen to produce heat and light. In that sense, fire is a bit like life. Our bodies also swap out virtually all their atoms roughly every seven years. But are you really your body? Because your body is made of atoms. And if you were your body, you'd be someone else every seven years. No — you too are a process, a reaction that sustains itself and...

Femke taps my shoulder. *Do you want more risotto, Wout?* What? Oh right, I was daydreaming. During our very pleasant but badly delayed Christmas dinner<sup>1</sup>, I realized I'd been staring at a candle for an uncomfortably long time. I added the topic to my list of exceptionally ordinary physics and decided I'd better find out how a candle flame actually works. Ready for the answer? Here we go!

### What happens when you light a candle?

When you light the wick, it's not actually the solid candle wax that burns first. You can tell, too — it usually takes a few seconds before a candle really catches. That's because the wax stored in the wick first has to melt, and then evaporate. Candle wax is made of paraffin, a mixture of very long carbon chains that can be up to 57 carbon atoms long. Most everyday fuels, like the petrol you put in a car, have much shorter chains.

Along the sides of those carbon chains sit hydrogen atoms that are only too eager to react with the oxygen in the air — a process we know as "combustion". But for that to happen, the wax vapour needs to be mixed with oxygen and heated enough. Oxygen is plentiful around the wick to begin with — after all, air is about 21% oxygen molecules. And the heat, of course, is supplied by the match you use to light your candle. You can already draw two logical conclusions from this story. First, it's much harder to light a candle in Antarctica than in Death Valley. Second, it's also much easier to light a candle inside an ISS module than a few metres outside the ISS.

And that's how that beautiful first little flame comes to be. In the seconds that follow, you'll notice the flame keeps growing. As soon as the wax in the wick is used up, the wick draws up fresh liquid wax, which is then melted by that same little flame. And the speed at which wax can melt depends on the size of the flame, since the flame supplies the necessary heat. And the size of the flame, in turn, depends on the speed at which wax is being melted, and so on... That positive feedback loop is what makes the flame keep growing. But the flame doesn't keep growing forever, because the rate at which the wick can draw up wax is limited. That drawing-up happens through a process called "capillary action". The wick is full of tiny fibres that pull the carbon chains along (via adhesion) and soak up the wax that way. That's a fairly slow process, and it doesn't take long before the wick can no longer keep up with the melting wax. A small pool of liquid wax then slowly builds up around the wick, and the flame stops growing.

### The different parts of a candle flame

As the flame grows bigger and stronger, the temperature inside it also rises. This gives rise to additional processes that create distinct zones within the flame. Have you ever wondered, for instance, why the bottom of a flame is blue? Well, it turns out that the blue base of the flame is not actually the hottest part. Did you know that? Let's take a look at these zones.

Right at the bottom of the candle, of course, is the liquid wax (A) that's slowly being drawn up. Just above it sits the wick, surrounded by a dead zone (B) that's heated by the surrounding flame to a temperature of around 600°C. I call it a "dead zone" because almost nothing happens here. No combustion takes place, and no light is emitted. This is where paraffin vapour leaves the wick and moves outward. This also pushes oxygen away, which is why no combustion can occur here yet. The only way to really make zone (B) visible is to blow the candle out — you'll then see a little stream of paraffin vapour rising up right next to the wick.

Just above the dead zone is a really interesting bit: the dark zone (C). Barely any light is emitted here either, but the temperature is so high (around 900°C) that the violent vibrations shake the carbon chains apart into fragments. At the molecular level, heat and vibration are, after all, one and the same thing. This process is called "pyrolysis". Pyrolysis produces much shorter carbon chains, but also various radicals (a fancy word for "unstable molecules") such as CH₂, CH₃ and C₂H₂. At the lower edge of the dark zone, where fresh oxygen enters the flame, those radicals then react with oxygen. They burn and form the molecule CH. But because that reaction releases a lot of energy, the electrons in this newly formed molecule end up in a higher energy state. And when those electrons then fall back down to a lower energy level, they emit light at one very specific wavelength: 431 nanometres. And do you know what colour that is? That's right, blue! And with that, we've discovered the next zone: the blue zone (D). This process — where a chemical reaction excites electrons, which then emit light as they relax — is also known as chemiluminescence. That, incidentally, is exactly why methane, propane and butane also burn with that same beautiful blue colour.

In the part of the dark zone that oxygen can't reach, however, something quite different happens. The smaller molecules produced by pyrolysis react with each other instead of with oxygen. In that process, clusters of carbon atoms build up, quickly growing to about 25 nanometres in size. In everyday life, we call these little clusters "soot". And those soot particles turn out to be crucial to how a candle works, especially in the next zone!

The next zone is the most important, and therefore also the brightest: the yellow zone (E). It sits on top of, and around, the dark zone, in the place where oxygen can finally get through. In effect, the glowing yellow zone forms a kind of hollow shell around zone D. When the soot particles reach this luminous zone, they burn up and get even hotter: a scorching 1200°C! For comparison, that's as hot as lava fresh out of a volcano! This makes the soot particles glow extremely brightly. It's that glow of the soot particles that makes a candle produce so much light.

You might think we've now covered every zone. But that's not quite true. Take another look at a candle flame. See that faint, translucent shell that seems to surround the flame? This is the very hottest part of the flame, reaching a temperature of no less than 1400°C! Let's call this zone (F) the "corona", after the similarly hot but thin — and therefore less bright — outer atmosphere of the Sun. In this zone, all the chemical reactions are finally completed, because this is the place where the most oxygen can reach the flame. When those soot particles formed, something else was left over too: the carbon atoms clump together into little soot balls, while the hydrogen atoms react to form hydrogen gas. That hydrogen gas burns in this outer corona, along with all the other leftover molecules such as CO, CH and C₂.

And there you have it — now you understand a candle so much better! Spread the word, I'd say. I never could have imagined that a candle is such a remarkable thing. From now on, I'll be looking at a burning candle with even more fascination. Stay curious!
